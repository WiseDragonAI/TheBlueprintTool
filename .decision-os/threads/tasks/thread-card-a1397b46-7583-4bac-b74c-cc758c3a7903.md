Short answer: yes, the internet has discussed this exact pattern, especially in AI logos. It is not a recognized academic “anus theory of logos.” The serious perceptual concept underneath the joke is **pareidolia**: the brain imposes a familiar object or body-part interpretation on ambiguous geometry. Research on pareidolia finds that animals, humans, faces, and body parts dominate the meanings people report in ambiguous images ([peer-reviewed study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10926141/)).

The logo pattern has a concrete visual recipe:

1. **Circular boundary.** The mark begins as a compact circle because circles communicate completeness, continuity, and friendliness.
2. **Central focal point.** Designers add a void, hub, portal, pupil, or convergence point to create depth and focus.
3. **Radial repetition.** Blades, folds, petals, rays, and interlocking segments rotate around that center to suggest intelligence, networks, emergence, and motion.
4. **Organic treatment.** Rounded curves and gradients soften the technology.

That same combination—outer boundary, central opening, radial folds, organic softness—is also the visual structure of a sphincter. The resemblance is therefore not mysterious: two different design intentions converge on the same primitive geometry. A 2025 article catalogued this exact pattern under the title [“Why do AI company logos look like buttholes?”](https://velvetshark.com/ai-company-logos-that-look-like-buttholes), and [Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2025/04/21/why-do-ai-company-logos-look-like-buttholes) amplified the observation.

There is also measurable design convergence. In 2023, a logo-design analysis found hexagons **4.6 times more common** in AI trademarks than in trademarks overall, with many AI marks adding rotation around a central point. The author argued that these logos increasingly signal membership in the AI category instead of creating a distinctive identity ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90869029/ai-boom-logo-trend-swirling-hexagon)). OpenAI’s own brand explanation explicitly associates circles with human warmth and right angles with technological precision, showing why this visual vocabulary keeps recurring ([OpenAI design guidelines](https://openai.com/brand/)).

The association is strengthened by cultural priming. Kurt Vonnegut famously used a simple asterisk-like radial drawing as an explicit illustration of an asshole in *Breakfast of Champions*. Once someone knows that joke, starbursts and radial AI marks become especially difficult to “unsee” in that way.

My conclusion for the compass question: **a compass does not inherently resemble an anus; a compass enclosed by a circle, organized around a visible hub, and softened with radial repetition recreates the exact high-risk recipe.** The two bare needles avoid it. Adding the enclosing circle and center hub brings it back.

Here are seven directional concepts that avoid the high-risk geometry: no circular enclosure, no center hub, no central void, no radial repetition, and no organic folds.

1. **Decision N — recommended.** Build the identity into the wordmark. Extend the diagonal stroke of the `N` in `DECISION` slightly toward the northeast and color only that extension. It communicates a bearing while remaining typographic, minimal, and distinctive.
2. **Route bend.** Use one continuous angular line that enters from the lower left, makes one decisive turn, and exits toward the upper right. The bend represents the decision; the exit represents direction.
3. **Bare bearing needle.** Use two narrow opposing triangles on a diagonal axis, with the northeast triangle longer and colored. Keep the gap between them empty and omit the circle and hub.
4. **Asymmetric fork.** Start with one straight path, split it into two unequal branches, and continue only the selected branch in the accent color. The asymmetry prevents a floral or anatomical reading.
5. **Ascending steps.** Use three short rectangular bars offset progressively upward and rightward. The mark communicates progress, sequence, and chosen trajectory without using an arrow.
6. **Open coordinates.** Use two separated right-angle corner marks, with the second shifted northeast. This suggests map coordinates, framing, and movement while never enclosing a center.
7. **Threshold crossing.** Place two short parallel bars as a boundary and pass one diagonal stroke through them. The symbol means crossing from deliberation into committed action.

My ranking for `DECISION OS` is:

1. **Decision N:** strongest brand distinctiveness and lowest anatomical risk.
2. **Route bend:** clearest combination of decision and direction.
3. **Bare bearing needle:** preserves the compass idea with the fewest possible shapes.

The governing visual rule is simple: **direction should come from an open trajectory, offset, extension, or asymmetry—not from rotation around a hole.**
